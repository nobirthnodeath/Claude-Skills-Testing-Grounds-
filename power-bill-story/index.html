An illustrated explainer on electricity prices

Who pays for the cloud?

Electric bills are rising faster than most of what you buy. A big part of the reason is being built down the road. Scroll to follow the power from the plant to your home, and see where the new costs go.

01 Your home

It starts with an ordinary house.

A typical American home uses about 900 kilowatt-hours of electricity a month. In 2020, the national average price was about 13.2 cents per kilowatt-hour. That made the bill about $119.

Watch the bill in the corner. It follows the national average price as this story moves forward in time.

02 The grid

Your bill pays for three things.

  • Making the power. Power plants burn fuel or catch the wind and sun.
  • Moving the power. High-voltage lines carry it across the region. Substations step it down for your street.
  • Keeping power in reserve. Grid operators pay plants to be ready for the hottest afternoon of the year. This is called capacity.

Homes and businesses share the cost of this system. When someone needs the grid to grow, everyone's rates can feel it.

03 A new neighbor

Then a data center moves in.

AI tools are trained and run in buildings full of computer chips. The chips run day and night, and they need constant cooling.

100+ MWA single large campus can draw more than 100 megawatts. That is about the average draw of 80,000 homes.

In 2023, data centers used 4.4% of all U.S. electricity. Federal researchers expect 6.7% to 12% by 2028.

Source: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory for the U.S. Dept. of Energy, Dec. 2024.

04 A crowded grid

Demand is growing faster than supply.

New power plants and lines take years to build. A data center can be ready in months. When demand jumps before supply catches up, the price of keeping power in reserve goes up.

PJM runs the grid for 67 million people in 13 states and D.C. Its independent market monitor says data center demand caused $29.4 billion, or 46%, of capacity charges over the last four auctions. Those charges reach household bills.

Sources: PJM auction results; Monitoring Analytics via Utility Dive, 2026.

05 The build-out

New wires. Shared bills.

To serve a data center, utilities build new substations and high-voltage lines. The utility adds that cost to its rates and earns a set return on it. So every customer pays a share, not only the data center.

Some tech companies also get special contracts with private terms. Harvard's Electricity Law Initiative reviewed about 50 utility proceedings. It found deals that can shift costs onto families and small businesses.

$31BRate increases that U.S. utilities asked for in 2025. That is double the year before.

Sources: Harvard ELI, “Extracting Profits from the Public,” 2025; PowerLines, 2026.

06 Back home

The same house. A bigger bill.

By April 2026, the national average price reached 18.8 cents per kilowatt-hour. For the same home, that is about $169 a month, or $50 more than in 2020.

Fuel prices, storms and old equipment add costs too. But researchers at Carnegie Mellon and NC State project that data center growth could raise U.S. power costs about 8% by 2030, and more than 25% in parts of Virginia.

Sources: U.S. EIA residential prices; Carnegie Mellon Open Energy Outlook, 2025.

07 A fairer deal

It does not have to work this way.

Data centers can grow without passing their costs to households. States and regulators can set clear rules:

  1. Make data centers pay their full cost.The companies that need new lines, substations and power plants pay for them.
  2. No secret deals.Every special contract between a utility and a data center is made public.
  3. Protect household bills.Regulators check the effect on home bills before they approve a large new load.

Some states have started. In 2025, Ohio regulators approved a rule that makes new data centers pay for most of the power they reserve, even if they use less.
